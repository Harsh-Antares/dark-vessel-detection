=======================================================================
 PART A — THE DOMAIN
=======================================================================

A1. What is SAR (Synthetic Aperture Radar)?
-------------------------------------------
A camera is a PASSIVE sensor: it records sunlight reflected off the
world, so it needs daylight and clear skies. Radar is an ACTIVE sensor:
the satellite transmits its own microwave pulses and records the echo
("backscatter"). Two consequences matter enormously here:
  * It works at night — it brings its own illumination.
  * Microwaves (Sentinel-1 uses C-band, ~5.5 cm wavelength) pass
    through clouds, rain, and smoke almost untouched.
So SAR images the ocean 24/7 in any weather. That is why it is the ONLY
sensor that can reliably see ships that don't want to be seen.

The "synthetic aperture" part: angular resolution of a radar antenna is
proportional to antenna size, and a satellite can't carry a kilometres-
long antenna. Instead, as the satellite flies, it records echoes along
its path and combines them in software AS IF they came from one giant
antenna — an aperture that is "synthesized" by motion. That's how a
700 km-high satellite achieves ~10 m ground resolution.

A2. Why do ships glow in SAR images?
------------------------------------
The open sea, slightly roughened by wind, scatters most microwave
energy AWAY from the satellite (like a slightly wavy mirror) — so ocean
looks dark. A metal ship is full of right-angled surfaces (hull-deck,
deck-superstructure). Right angles act as CORNER REFLECTORS: they bounce
energy straight back where it came from, like a bicycle reflector. So a
metal vessel appears as a small cluster of very bright pixels against a
dark background. That contrast is the physical signal our detector
learns.

Caveats the project handles:
  * Wind roughens the sea and raises background brightness ("sea
    clutter") — that's why wind speed is fed to the model as an input
    channel: it tells the network how bright "empty ocean" should look.
  * Fixed metal infrastructure (wind turbines, oil platforms, buoys)
    also glints like a ship — that's why there's a dedicated
    vessel-vs-non-vessel classification head.

A3. VV and VH polarisation — why two channels?
----------------------------------------------
Microwaves are transverse waves with an orientation (polarisation).
Sentinel-1 transmits Vertically polarised pulses and records the echo
in two ways:
  * VV — transmitted Vertical, received Vertical.
  * VH — transmitted Vertical, received Horizontal ("cross-pol").
Simple single-bounce surfaces (the sea) mostly preserve polarisation,
so they show up in VV. Complex multi-bounce structures (ship
superstructures) rotate some of the energy into the horizontal plane,
so they show up relatively brighter in VH. In practice: VH has less sea
clutter and makes ships pop; VV carries complementary texture. Feeding
both channels lets the network exploit the difference — analogous to
how colour helps more than greyscale.

A4. Decibels and why SAR data is "heavy-tailed"
-----------------------------------------------
Raw backscatter intensities span many orders of magnitude — sea might
return 0.001 while a ship's corner reflector returns 10. A neural
network hates inputs like that. The standard fix is the decibel scale:
    value_dB = 10 * log10(intensity)
Logarithms compress huge ranges into manageable ones. xView3 ships its
imagery already in dB; we then clip to [-35, 0] dB (below -35 is noise,
0 dB is already extremely bright) and rescale to [0, 1].
This is the single most important normalisation decision in the
pipeline (see tiling.py, _clip_scale).

A5. Speckle
-----------
SAR images look "grainy" even over uniform sea. This is speckle: within
each pixel, many tiny wave reflections interfere constructively or
destructively at random. It is multiplicative noise inherent to all
coherent imaging (lasers do it too). We don't explicitly de-speckle —
the convolutional network learns to average over it — but it's why a
single bright pixel is NOT yet a ship, and why detection of 3-pixel
targets is genuinely hard.

A6. AIS, and what "dark vessel" really means
--------------------------------------------
AIS (Automatic Identification System) is a VHF radio transponder that
broadcasts a ship's identity (MMSI number), position, course, and
speed, mainly for collision avoidance. International rules require it
on larger vessels — but the captain can switch it off, and those who
fish illegally do.

A "dark vessel" = a vessel physically present (radar sees it) that is
not broadcasting AIS (the database doesn't). READ THIS TWICE:
darkness is a property of the RELATIONSHIP between two data sources,
not of the radar image. No model can look at a SAR chip and "see" that
AIS is off — a dark trawler and a cooperative trawler are pixel-
identical. The dark label is produced by DATA FUSION (Part F). Stating
this clearly in your write-ups is a maturity signal; claiming a CNN
detects "darkness" visually is a classic rookie overclaim.

A7. The dataset: xView3-SAR
---------------------------
~1,000 Sentinel-1 IW GRD scenes (~29,000 x 24,000 px, 10 m/pixel), each
with: VH_dB and VV_dB SAR channels; co-registered bathymetry (sea
depth), wind speed/direction/quality, and a land/ice mask; plus 220k+
labels as POINTS (scene row/col), each carrying is_vessel, is_fishing,
vessel_length_m, distance_from_shore_km, and a confidence grade.
The labels were created by matching AIS to SAR: HIGH-confidence =
AIS-correlated (identity known => cooperative), MEDIUM = visually
confirmed in radar but no AIS match (=> our dark ground truth), LOW =
uncertain (we exclude them). The validation split has the cleanest
labels — mine it.


=======================================================================
 PART B — GEOSPATIAL ENGINEERING  (data/tiling.py, scripts/02)
=======================================================================

B1. Rasters, GeoTIFFs, and the geotransform
-------------------------------------------
A raster is a grid of pixels; a GeoTIFF is a raster plus metadata that
pins the grid to the Earth: a coordinate reference system (CRS) and an
AFFINE GEOTRANSFORM — six numbers mapping pixel (row, col) to map
coordinates (x, y):
    x = a*col + b*row + c
    y = d*col + e*row + f
(c, f) is the top-left corner; a and e are pixel width/height in map
units. This little matrix is what lets us say "the bright blob at pixel
(14302, 9871) is at 11.42E, 42.18N". Lose it and your detections are
just numbers; keep it and they're evidence you can put on a chart.
In code: rasterio exposes it as dataset.transform, and
SceneReader.pixel_to_lonlat() applies it (plus a projection conversion
to WGS84 lat/lon if the scene is stored in a projected CRS like UTM).

B2. CRS in one paragraph
------------------------
The Earth is a lumpy ellipsoid; maps are flat. A CRS is the contract
for flattening. EPSG:4326 (WGS84) = raw longitude/latitude in degrees —
great for storage, terrible for measuring distance (a degree of
longitude shrinks toward the poles). UTM zones = local metre-based
projections, good for distances. Equal-area projections (we use World
Mollweide, ESRI:54009) preserve AREA — which is why mpa.py reprojects
MPA polygons to Mollweide before computing km^2. Computing "area" in
degrees is a meaningless number; this is one of the most common GIS
bugs in the wild.

B3. Windowed reads — handling rasters bigger than RAM
-----------------------------------------------------
A 29,000 x 24,000 float32 raster is ~2.8 GB per channel; with 5+
channels you'd need ~15 GB just to LOOK at one scene. The fix: GeoTIFFs
are internally chunked, so you can read any rectangular window straight
off disk without touching the rest:
    src.read(1, window=Window(col_off, row_off, w, h))
SceneReader._read_window does exactly this (with boundless=True to pad
at scene edges). The whole pipeline — tiling AND inference — never
holds a full scene in memory. This pattern (windowed/streamed I/O over
data that doesn't fit) is a transferable engineering skill far beyond
remote sensing.

B4. Overlapping tiles and the seam problem
------------------------------------------
We cut scenes into 1024x1024 tiles. If tiles touched edge-to-edge, a
vessel sitting exactly on the boundary would be half in each tile and
might be missed in both. So adjacent tiles OVERLAP by 128 px: every
vessel appears fully inside at least one tile. The cost: a vessel in
the overlap strip is detected twice. The cure is geographic NMS at
inference (Part E3). iter_tile_specs() implements the overlapping grid;
note how the last row/column is shifted back so tiles never run off the
scene.

B5. Normalisation of each input channel
---------------------------------------
Networks train best when inputs live in a similar, bounded range:
  * SAR dB: clip [-35, 0] -> [0, 1]   (Part A4)
  * Bathymetry: clip [-6000, 100] m -> [0, 1]. Depth matters because
    most fishing happens over shelves and banks — the network can learn
    "vessels here are plausible fishers".
  * Wind speed: clip [0, 30] m/s -> [0, 1]. Tells the model the
    expected clutter level.
  * NaNs (nodata) are filled with 0 after scaling.
The land/ice mask is NOT fed to the model as a channel here; it's used
to (a) drop near-pure-land tiles and (b) hard-zero the output heatmap
over land at inference. A hard rule beats a learned rule when the rule
is exact.

B6. Sparsity and positive-aware sampling
----------------------------------------
The ocean is mostly empty: the vast majority of tiles contain zero
vessels. Two interventions:
  * At tiling time we keep ALL tiles containing labels but only ~5% of
    empty tiles (config: empty_tile_keep_prob) — the rest teach the
    model nothing and waste disk.
  * At training time, PositiveAwareSampler (dataset.py) composes each
    epoch so ~75% of sampled chips contain at least one vessel. Without
    this, batches are almost all background and the gradient signal
    from rare positives drowns.
General lesson: when positives are rare, you control the data
distribution the model sees — you don't accept the natural one.


=======================================================================
 PART C — THE DEEP LEARNING  (models/model.py)
=======================================================================

C1. Convolutional networks in three paragraphs
----------------------------------------------
A convolution slides a small learnable filter (say 3x3) across the
image, producing a map of "how much does each location look like this
pattern". Early layers learn edges and blobs; deeper layers compose
them into textures and object parts. Three properties make convolutions
ideal for imagery: locality (nearby pixels relate), weight sharing (a
ship-pattern detector works anywhere in the image), and translation
equivariance (shift the input, the output shifts).

Downsampling (strided convs / pooling) makes deeper layers see LARGER
context per output cell at LOWER spatial resolution. A classification
net ends with global pooling — "what is in this image" — throwing
position away entirely. Detection needs the opposite: we must know
WHERE, to 10-metre precision.

That tension — deep semantic features vs fine spatial precision — is
the central design problem of dense prediction, and the U-Net is its
classic resolution.

C2. The U-Net: encoder, decoder, skip connections
-------------------------------------------------
Shape: a "U". The ENCODER (left side) is a standard CNN (here, a
ResNet) that downsamples 1/2 -> 1/4 -> ... -> 1/32 while growing
channel depth: it builds "what is here" understanding. The DECODER
(right side) upsamples step by step back to full resolution. The trick
is the SKIP CONNECTIONS: at each decoder step, the upsampled deep
features are CONCATENATED with the encoder features of the same
resolution. The decoder therefore gets both the semantics ("this region
has ship-like context") and the crisp spatial detail ("the bright pixel
is exactly HERE"). For 3-pixel targets, those skip connections aren't a
nicety — without them the precise location is unrecoverable after 32x
downsampling.
In code: ResNetEncoder returns five feature scales; UNetDecoder._up_cat
does bilinear-upsample + concatenate; each up-block is two 3x3 convs.

C3. ResNets and transfer learning on radar
------------------------------------------
A ResNet stacks residual blocks: each block computes F(x) and outputs
x + F(x). The identity path lets gradients flow through dozens of
layers without vanishing — the innovation that made very deep nets
trainable (He et al., 2015).
We initialise the encoder with ImageNet-pretrained weights. But
ImageNet is 3-channel RGB photos and we have 4-channel radar+ancillary
stacks. The standard adaptation (ResNetEncoder.__init__): rebuild the
first conv for 4 channels, initialising each new channel with the MEAN
of the pretrained RGB filters. Why does ImageNet help on radar at all?
Because the earliest filters are generic edge/blob/texture detectors,
useful for any imagery; the network fine-tunes the rest. Pretraining
mostly buys faster convergence and better behaviour when labelled data
is limited.

C4. Why heatmaps instead of bounding boxes — the core modelling choice
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Classic detectors (Faster R-CNN, YOLO, SSD) predict BOXES, mostly by
classifying and refining pre-placed "anchor" boxes of various sizes.
That machinery assumes objects have meaningful extent and shape. An
xView3 vessel is ~3-20 px — effectively a POINT (the labels themselves
are points!). For point-like targets, anchors are pure overhead: sizes
to tune, positive/negative IoU thresholds that misbehave for tiny
boxes, NMS over thousands of proposals.

The CenterNet/keypoint alternative reframes detection as DENSE
REGRESSION: predict one full-resolution map where the value at each
pixel is "the probability a vessel is centred here". Training targets:
render a small 2-D Gaussian (sigma ~2.5 px) at every true vessel
location (dataset.py: gaussian_splat — note overlapping vessels take
the per-pixel MAX, keeping nearby peaks distinct). Inference: find
local maxima (Part E1). No anchors, no box hyper-parameters, graceful
handling of dense clusters of small boats. This matches what the
strongest xView3 competition solutions did.

Why a Gaussian rather than a single hot pixel? Tolerance: a prediction
1 px off a true centre is nearly correct, and the soft target says so
— it also feeds the "penalty-reduced" part of the focal loss (D2).

C5. The attribute heads and multi-task learning
-----------------------------------------------
On the shared decoder output, four tiny heads (3x3 conv -> ReLU -> 1x1
conv) each produce a full-resolution map:
    heatmap — vessel-centre confidence (the detector)
    vessel  — P(vessel | something here): rejects turbines/platforms
    fishing — P(fishing vessel)
    length  — predicted log(vessel length in metres)
The attribute maps are only READ AT POINTS: during training, at ground-
truth centres (gather_at_points); at inference, at detected peaks. This
is the standard CenterNet pattern — "predict everywhere, supervise at
keypoints".
Multi-task learning means one backbone optimised for several objectives
at once (a weighted loss sum, Part D4). Benefits: shared features
become more general (length cues help vessel-ness; fishing-vessel shape
helps both), one model to deploy instead of four, and regularisation —
each task constrains the others against overfitting.
Why log-length? Lengths span ~5 m to ~300 m; in log space the target is
roughly Gaussian and a fixed absolute error means a fixed RELATIVE
error, which matches how the metric scores it.

C6. A small detail with a big effect: heatmap bias initialisation
-----------------------------------------------------------------
DarkVesselNet initialises the heatmap head's final bias to -4.6, i.e.
sigmoid ~0.01. Untrained networks otherwise start predicting ~0.5
everywhere; with 99.999% background, the first epochs would be spent
just learning "almost everything is background", with huge unstable
losses. Starting at the true base rate skips that. (Trick from the
RetinaNet/focal-loss paper.)


=======================================================================
 PART D — TRAINING MECHANICS  (models/losses.py, train.py)
=======================================================================

D1. The class-imbalance problem, quantified
-------------------------------------------
A 1024x1024 tile has 1,048,576 pixels; a vessel occupies ~10. Even a
tile with 5 vessels is 99.995% background. With plain binary cross-
entropy, background dominates the loss ~20,000:1 — the model learns to
output 0 everywhere and the loss still looks great. Every design choice
in this part exists to defeat that ratio.

D2. Focal loss, derived in steps  (heatmap_focal_loss)
------------------------------------------------------
Start from BCE for one pixel: -log(p) if positive, -log(1-p) if
negative. Problem: millions of EASY negatives (open ocean, p already
~0.001) each contribute a tiny loss, but their sheer number swamps the
few positives.
Step 1 — the focal term (Lin et al., 2017): multiply by how WRONG the
model is:  positives get (1-p)^alpha * -log(p); negatives get
p^alpha * -log(1-p), with alpha=2. An easy negative with p=0.01
now contributes 0.0001x its BCE loss — effectively muted. Gradient
concentrates on hard examples.
Step 2 — the penalty-reduction term (CornerNet): pixels NEAR a true
centre aren't really negatives; punishing the model for firing 2 px off
a vessel is counterproductive. Our Gaussian target y encodes nearness,
so negatives are additionally weighted by (1-y)^beta (beta=4): full
penalty far from any vessel, almost none right next to a peak.
Step 3 — normalise by the NUMBER OF POSITIVES (not pixels), so a tile's
loss scale doesn't depend on how empty it is.
That's the whole loss — read heatmap_focal_loss line by line now and
it should map 1:1 to these steps.

D3. Masked attribute losses
---------------------------
xView3 labels have honest gaps: is_fishing is often unknown (NaN), the
length too. We must not invent targets. Each attribute gets a MASK
(1 = known, 0 = unknown); the per-point loss is multiplied by the mask
and divided by mask.sum() — unknowns contribute exactly zero gradient
(masked_point_bce / masked_point_l1). For length we use Smooth-L1
(Huber): quadratic for small errors, linear for big ones, so one
mislabelled 300 m tanker doesn't blow up the gradients like plain MSE
would.

D4. Combining the tasks
-----------------------
total = 1.0*heatmap + 0.5*vessel + 0.5*fishing + 0.2*length
The weights encode priority: detection is the mission; attributes
refine it. Symptoms to watch when YOU tune them: if detection F1 is
fine but length error is terrible, raise w_length; if the heatmap
degrades when attributes are added, lower theirs. (Fancier schemes —
uncertainty weighting, GradNorm — exist; fixed weights are fine here.)

D5. The optimisation stack, briefly
-----------------------------------
  * AdamW: Adam (per-parameter adaptive learning rates from gradient
    moment estimates) with weight decay applied correctly (decoupled
    from the adaptive scaling). Default optimiser for vision today.
  * Cosine learning-rate schedule: LR decays smoothly from 3e-4 to ~0
    over training — big steps early, gentle polishing late.
  * Mixed precision (autocast + GradScaler): compute in float16 where
    safe, keep master weights in float32, scale the loss so tiny fp16
    gradients don't underflow to zero. ~2x faster, ~half the memory,
    same accuracy. Free performance on modern GPUs.
  * Gradient accumulation: if GPU memory limits you to batch 4, run N
    micro-batches, summing gradients, and step the optimiser every N —
    mathematically a batch of 4N.
  * Augmentation (dataset.py): random flips and 90-degree rotations.
    Safe because open ocean has no preferred orientation at this scale;
    each labelled point's coordinates are transformed with the image.
    Effectively multiplies the dataset 8x.

D6. Watching the right number during training
---------------------------------------------
train.py evaluates a quick detection P/R/F1 on validation chips every
epoch (peak extraction + greedy matching within 20 px = 200 m), and
checkpoints the best F1, not the best loss. Loss and metric usually
agree, but when they diverge, the metric is the truth. Expect F1 ~0 for
the first epochs (threshold not yet exceeded) — that's normal.


=======================================================================
 PART E — INFERENCE  (inference/peaks.py, nms.py, predict.py)
=======================================================================

E1. Peak extraction — the 5-line detector head
----------------------------------------------
A detection = a local maximum of the predicted heatmap above a score
threshold. Implementation trick: run a 3x3 maximum filter over the
heatmap; a pixel that EQUALS its own neighbourhood-max IS the local
max. Two lines of scipy replace the entire proposal/NMS machinery of
box detectors. The score threshold is your precision/recall dial: for
dark-vessel work you bias toward recall (lower threshold) — a false
alarm costs an analyst a glance; a missed dark vessel is the mission
failing.

E2. Sliding-window scene inference
----------------------------------
predict_scene() walks the same overlapping tile grid as training,
reads each window off disk, predicts, extracts peaks, and converts
peak coordinates: tile (row, col) + tile offset -> scene (row, col) ->
geotransform -> lon/lat. Land pixels are hard-zeroed in the heatmap
first (the mask is exact knowledge — don't ask the network to relearn
the coastline). Tiles that are >90% land are skipped entirely.

E3. Geographic NMS — de-duplicating across seams
------------------------------------------------
Because tiles overlap, one vessel near a seam is detected by 2-4 tiles.
These duplicates are in DIFFERENT tiles, so per-tile suppression can't
see them; we de-duplicate in SCENE coordinates: sort all detections by
score; repeatedly keep the best remaining one and suppress every other
detection within 100 m of it. A k-d tree (a binary space-partitioning
structure giving O(log n) radius queries) makes this O(n log n) —
worth knowing: k-d trees are THE tool for "what's near this point"
problems, and they reappear in the AIS matching and the evaluation.

E4. What each final detection carries
-------------------------------------
scene_id, scene row/col, lon/lat, heatmap score, P(vessel),
P(fishing), estimated length in metres — i.e. everything downstream
fusion and analysis need, in one CSV row per ship.


=======================================================================
 PART F — DATA FUSION: HOW "DARK" IS DECIDED  (fusion/ais_correlation.py)
=======================================================================

F1. The principle
-----------------
Two independent observations of the same ocean:
   SAR:  "something metal is at (lat, lon) at time T"     (physics)
   AIS:  "I am vessel #123 at (lat, lon) at time T"       (self-report)
Correlate them: a SAR detection WITH a matching AIS report within a
space-time window is COOPERATIVE; one with NO match is DARK. The
intelligence is in the discrepancy. All "dark vessel detection"
systems, including Global Fishing Watch's, are built on this exact
join.

F2. Space-time matching done honestly (live mode)
-------------------------------------------------
AIS messages arrive irregularly (seconds to minutes apart), and the SAR
snapshot is one instant. So for each AIS track (grouped by MMSI), we
LINEARLY INTERPOLATE position to the SAR acquisition time (between the
last fix before and first fix after), then match detections to
interpolated positions within a radius (~500 m — generous because ships
move and both sources have positional error). Matching uses the same
k-d tree pattern as NMS. Edge cases handled: a track with fixes only
before (or after) the snapshot is used only if the gap is small;
detections matching nothing stay dark; if NO usable AIS exists,
everything is "dark" and the code's comment says: report that loudly,
not silently.

F3. xView3 mode (what the scripts use)
--------------------------------------
The dataset's makers already did F2 against historical AIS when
building the labels: confidence HIGH = AIS-correlated (cooperative),
MEDIUM = visually-confirmed vessel with no AIS match (dark). So
transfer_dark_labels() nearest-neighbour-matches OUR detections to
ground-truth points (200 m) and inherits the status. Detections
matching no label get is_dark = NaN — "unknown", kept distinct from
both classes. Preserving a three-way dark/cooperative/unknown split
instead of force-classifying everything is the kind of honesty the
evaluation rewards.

A note on local distance computation: to measure metres between
lon/lat points over a <100 km extent, we use a local equirectangular
projection — x = R*lon*cos(lat0), y = R*lat (radians). Within a scene
the error is negligible, and it's 100x cheaper than exact geodesics.
Know when approximations are safe; document them when you use them.

F4. The optional risk prior — and its careful framing
-----------------------------------------------------
fit_dark_prior() trains a logistic regression P(dark | length,
P(fishing), score), wrapped in a probability CALIBRATION step
(CalibratedClassifierCV): raw classifier scores are often not true
probabilities; calibration fixes the mapping so "0.8" means "right 80%
of the time". Use: RANKING detections for analyst triage when AIS is
unavailable. Never say it identifies dark vessels — it encodes priors
like "small fishing vessels far from port are more often dark". The
difference between a prior and an identification is exactly the
difference between defensible analytics and an overclaim.


=======================================================================
 PART G — FROM DETECTIONS TO A DECISION PRODUCT  (analysis/, visualization/)
=======================================================================

G1. Spatial joins (analysis/mpa.py)
-----------------------------------
"Which detections are inside which protected area?" is a point-in-
polygon test, done at scale by geopandas' sjoin with an R-tree spatial
index (the polygon analogue of the k-d tree). MPA polygons come from
the WDPA (Protected Planet) database. Output per MPA: total/dark/
cooperative counts, the dark ratio, and dark density per km^2 — with
areas computed in an equal-area projection (B2!).

G2. Snapshots to hours — the extrapolation, stated plainly
----------------------------------------------------------
What we HAVE: k dark vessels visible in an MPA at a few instants (one
per SAR pass, days apart). What people ASK for: "how much illegal
fishing happens here?" Bridging that gap requires assumptions, and the
whole game is making them EXPLICIT:
  Model: dark-vessel presence is roughly stationary over the window,
  and each pass is an unbiased instantaneous sample. Then the per-pass
  count estimates lambda = average number of dark vessels present at
  any moment, and over a window of T hours:
        estimated dark-vessel-hours H = lambda * T.
  (Intuition: lambda vessels present on average, for T hours.)
  Corrections: divide counts by the detector's measured DARK RECALL —
  if you only catch 80% of dark vessels, the truth is count/0.8. An
  explicit, measured correction, not a fudge.
This mirrors Global Fishing Watch's "apparent fishing effort" framing.
And the language matters: these are PRESENCE-hours of dark vessels —
strong evidence of illegal-fishing pressure, not a court-ready measure
of fishing time.

G3. Uncertainty: bootstrap and a Bayesian fallback (analysis/effort.py)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
A single number ("4,200 illegal fishing hours!") invites deserved
skepticism; an interval invites trust.
  * THE BOOTSTRAP: with n per-pass counts, resample n counts WITH
    replacement many times; each resample's mean is one plausible
    lambda. The 2.5th-97.5th percentiles of those means form a 95%
    confidence interval. No distributional assumptions, three lines of
    numpy, works for almost any statistic — if you learn one
    statistical tool from this project, learn this one.
  * Tiny-sample fallback (n < 3): bootstrap degenerates, so we use the
    exact Bayesian result — for Poisson counts with a Jeffreys prior,
    the rate's posterior is Gamma(total_count + 0.5, n) — and sample
    from it instead.
The interval is then carried through to hours (H = lambda*T) and
printed on the map itself. Reviewers respect "1,800 [900-3,100] hours,
assumptions attached" far more than any confident single number.

G4. The map (visualization/maps.py)
-----------------------------------
folium writes Leaflet.js interactive maps from Python. Layers: MPA
polygons (green), cooperative detections (blue), dark detections
(red), unknown (grey), a heat-surface of dark density, and a banner
with the hours estimate ± CI. Each marker's popup carries its score,
P(vessel), P(fishing), and length. Design intent: a non-expert should
open pressure_map.html and within ten seconds understand "red dots =
ships hiding inside a protected area". That one artifact communicates
the entire project better than any table.


=======================================================================
 PART H — EVALUATION  (evaluation/metrics.py)
=======================================================================

H1. Matching detections to truth
--------------------------------
With point detections, "correct" means "within 200 m of an unclaimed
truth point" (20 px at 10 m/px — xView3's official radius). Greedy
1-to-1 matching in descending score order: each truth point can be
claimed once; a second detection on the same ship is a false positive
(this is what punishes seam duplicates if your NMS is weak). From the
matching: TP, FP, FN.

H2. Precision, recall, F1 — and which errors cost what
------------------------------------------------------
precision = TP/(TP+FP): of my alarms, how many were real?
recall    = TP/(TP+FN): of the real ships, how many did I find?
F1 = harmonic mean — punishes imbalance between them (arithmetic mean
of 1.0 and 0.0 is 0.5; harmonic is 0). But the F1 treats both error
types as equally costly, and in THIS mission they aren't: a missed
dark vessel is the system failing at its purpose; a false positive
costs a second look. Hence the breakdown:
  * DARK RECALL — recall computed only over dark truth vessels. The
    single most mission-relevant number, and the one standard
    leaderboard metrics ignore.
  * CLOSE-TO-SHORE recall (<2 km) — coastal clutter (rocks, surf,
    structures) is the hardest regime; models that look great offshore
    often collapse here; report it separately so the failure mode is
    visible.
  * VESSEL/NON-VESSEL and FISHING F1 on matched detections — the
    attribute heads' quality.
  * LENGTH SCORE — xView3-style: 1 minus mean absolute percentage
    error (so 1.0 is perfect).
  * An xView3-style AGGREGATE combining the above, so you have one
    comparable headline alongside the slices that carry the mission.

H3. The meta-lesson
-------------------
Choosing metrics IS choosing what the system optimises for. "Report
more than a single F1" is not bureaucracy — single-number leaderboards
actively hide mission-critical failures (a detector with stellar
overall F1 can have terrible dark recall if dark vessels are smaller
on average — which they are). Designing the metric slice that exposes
this is exactly the judgement employers are probing for when they ask
"how did you evaluate it?".
